The Shape of a Plate, In Atlanta

A 10-minute drive across this city is the difference between a full grocery aisle and a gas-station shelf. Here is what 530 census tracts, 1.8 million people, and one map of fresh food look like, one chapter at a time.

Half of Atlanta's neighborhoods have a Walmart or a Kroger within a mile. The other half have a Family Dollar and a McDonald's. The line between the two is not random — it tracks income, history, transit, and a half-century of supermarket retreat.

Where you can buy a tomato should not be a privilege. In Atlanta, it often is.

This story walks through five views of the city, in order. Each map is live — pan, zoom, and click any neighborhood to see its numbers. The narrative tells you what to look for; the map shows you why.

Chapter One

The Big Picture

The first map shows Atlanta as a food system. Red polygons are USDA-designated food deserts — neighborhoods where the typical family is more than a mile from a full-service supermarket and earns below the regional median. Grocery stores, hospitals, schools, and MARTA bus routes overlay the same tracts.

Look at the red shape: it forms a crescent through southwest and west Atlanta — the same crescent civil-rights historians have called the Black Belt of Atlanta. Now look at where the green grocery dots cluster: north and east, almost mirror-imaged.

The map answers a question that statistics alone cannot: where. A 16% food-desert rate is an abstraction; this crescent is a place. It is also the through-line of the next four chapters.

Chapter Two

The Grocery Gap

The USDA's working definition of "food access" in an urban area is one mile from a supermarket. Draw a one-mile circle around every supermarket and farmers market in metro Atlanta and you get the green halo below. Anything outside the green is, technically, a food desert candidate.

228 of 530 Tracts (43%) that fall outside
the 1-mile fresh-food halo

The Modified Retail Food Environment Index (mRFEI) measures the same imbalance numerically. It counts healthy retailers (supermarkets, farmers markets) as a fraction of all food retailers in a tract. A score below 10 is a "food swamp" — fast food and convenience stores dominate. The Atlanta region's average mRFEI is 38, and a sharper number tells the deeper story:

30× A non-LILA tract holds, on average, 30 times more grocery stores than a LILA tract (0.60 vs 0.02 per tract). For supermarkets specifically, the ratio is 5×.

Chapter Three

Neighborhoods Left Behind

Distance to a grocery is one piece. Income is another. A composite "Food Access Gap Score" combines four factors — distance, mRFEI, vehicle access, and poverty — into a single 0-to-100 ramp. Darker red means a tighter gap.

This is the map that gets used in policy briefs. The dark-red tracts are where USDA food-desert status, low income, no-car households, and a missing supermarket all overlap. There are 49 such tracts in the Severe or Critical band.

49 Tracts in the Severe or Critical
Food Access Gap band

It is tempting to call this an income story. It is partly one — the gap score correlates with poverty rate at r = 0.38 and with median income at r = −0.34. But the data also surfaces a less obvious pattern.

It's not the fast food. It's the convenience stores.

The popular narrative says food deserts get filled with fast-food restaurants. The Atlanta data tells a more nuanced story: LILA tracts actually have fewer fast-food locations than non-LILA tracts (0.63 vs 1.00 per tract). What they have 1.6× more of is corner convenience and dollar stores — the everyday substitutes that take a tract's mRFEI from "imbalanced" to "swamp."

+4.4 In an OLS regression, every additional convenience or dollar store in a tract is associated with a 4.4-point increase in the Food Access Gap Score. Each additional supermarket is worth a 9.7-point decrease (n = 519, R² = 0.31).

Chapter Four

The Last-Mile Problem

Roughly 100,000 people in Fulton and DeKalb live in households with no car. For them, "the nearest supermarket is 1.4 miles away" is not a one-way Uber trip — it's a 30-minute walk each way with bags, or a bus transfer that may add 45 minutes.

100,265 People in metro Atlanta living
in households with no vehicle

The transit story has a workaround that planners increasingly favor: MARTA Markets, mobile pantries, and corner-store-conversion grants. The transit map shows you where to focus those interventions — exactly the bus stops that pass through deep-red tracts.

r = 0.54 % of households with no vehicle correlates with the CDC food-insecurity rate at r = 0.54 — one of the strongest single predictors at the tract level. ANOVA across the five gap-label groups confirms the effect is highly significant (F = 11.6, p < 10⁻⁸).

Chapter Five

The Health Toll & the Network Already Here

Food access is not just an inconvenience. It bends a city's health curve. The CDC's PLACES dataset estimates tract-level food insecurity rate directly from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System. Overlay it with chronic-disease prevalence and the lines converge.

But Atlanta is not waiting for federal action. A parallel food network — pantries, faith-based distributors, urban farms, and restaurants like Goodr — has stitched itself across the same crescent. The pin map below shows where it lives.

Because the indicator measures retail food access — supermarkets, farmers markets, and grocery distance — not whether undergraduates can swipe into a dining hall. The tract has no supermarket, two convenience stores, one fast-food location, and roughly 1 in 4 households without a car. By the same metric the rest of this essay uses, our home tract is in the bottom decile of metro Atlanta.

That's a feature, not an embarrassment. The shape of the gap is the same in our backyard as it is in West End, Bankhead, or southwest College Park: a missing supermarket, an overrepresentation of corner stores, and a transit-dependent population. The remedies are also the same. We work on a campus food map; we live inside its data.

Chapter Six

By the Numbers

The five maps above are visual evidence. The numbers below are statistical evidence — Pearson correlations, one-way ANOVA, Welch t-tests, and OLS regression run across all 530 tracts in the study area. We use the same dataset that powers the maps, so every coefficient ties back to a polygon you can click.

Three findings stand out:

  1. Food insecurity is a chronic-disease story. Tract-level food-insecurity rate (CDC PLACES) correlates with obesity at r = 0.92 and with diabetes at r = 0.81. Translation: an obesity-rate map of Atlanta and a food-insecurity map of Atlanta look almost identical. The exposure precedes the disease — and the disease compounds the exposure.
  2. The Food Access Gap Score has many small drivers, not one big one. Poverty (r = 0.38), obesity (r = 0.37), convenience-store count (r = 0.37), and median income (r = -0.34) all sit within a tight band — meaning a one-variable explanation is wrong. Our OLS model with five predictors lands at R² = 0.31; doubling the predictor list (adding chronic-disease covariates) pushes it to 0.50+.
  3. LILA status is a 1.5-σ shift on chronic disease. Welch t-tests with Cohen's d as effect size yield d = 1.55 for obesity, 1.35 for diabetes, and 1.14 for food insecurity. These are not statistical curiosities — they are some of the largest sociospatial effect sizes you will see in public-health data.

The regression result is the most actionable. If a planner wants to predict where the next gap will open, the variables that matter are: convenience-store concentration, supermarket retreat, and vehicle access. Income and unemployment matter too, but they are downstream — they tell you who is affected, not where the next gap is forming.

R² = 0.61 For predicting food insecurity, a six-variable OLS model (gap score + four POI counts + transit + income) explains 61% of variance across 519 tracts. The single biggest coefficient is % no-vehicle (β = +0.36 per percentage point).

Chapter Seven

Where you live matters — beyond what you earn.

The cleanest way to test "place vs. paycheck" is to do it directly. Fit a regression of food insecurity on income and poverty alone. Then add 40 neighborhood fixed-effects on top — one binary for every neighborhood with at least three tracts — and ask whether the model improves.

+11.4 pts Adding neighborhood fixed-effects raises explained variance in food insecurity from R² = 0.69 to R² = 0.80 — an 11.4-percentage-point gain over income and poverty alone (partial F = 7.06, p < 0.001).

Translation: even after controlling for how much a household earns and how poor its block is, knowing which neighborhood it sits in still adds genuinely new predictive power. Place is not a proxy for pocketbook. It carries its own signal — built environment, retail history, transit catchment, redlining legacy.

The same conclusion shows up in spatial-statistics terms. Moran's I — the standard test for spatial clustering — is positive and highly significant for every key variable in this study:

    A Moran's I of 0.64 for food insecurity, in particular, is striking. It says food-insecure tracts are surrounded by other food-insecure tracts at a level far above chance. The condition spreads block by block, not house by house.

    Two tracts, same income, different gap.

    The clearest demonstration that geography is more than economics: pairs of tracts with nearly identical median income but radically different access. The income band brackets each pair to ±$5,000.
